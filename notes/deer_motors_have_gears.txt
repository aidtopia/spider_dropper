Your theory is quite plausible, and the answer depends on which part of the assembly you're calling the motor.

The actual AC synchronous motor inside these "reindeer motors" almost certainly does not contain reduction gears. The motor itself is usually just:

A stator energized directly from 120 V AC.

A permanent magnet (or hysteresis-type) rotor that locks to the AC line frequency.

Bearings or bushings.

That motor spins much too fast to directly produce 6 RPM. On 60 Hz power, a common synchronous motor might run at 1800 RPM, 3600 RPM, or another speed depending on its pole count. Something has to reduce that speed by hundreds of times.

The reduction is almost always accomplished by a gear train attached to the motor, typically enclosed in the same crimped metal can. Manufacturers often sell the whole assembly as a "synchronous motor," even though it's really a synchronous motor plus gearbox.

The five little pins you noticed are a strong clue. They are very likely the ends of shafts supporting the intermediate gears.

A typical construction looks something like this:

Rotor
  │
Small pinion
  │
[Gear 1]
  │
[Gear 2]
  │
[Gear 3]
  │
...
  │
Output shaft (6 RPM)
Those little gear shafts often protrude through the stamped metal plate exactly as you described.

Why the ticking?
Several failure modes are possible after a jam.

1. Stripped or chipped gear teeth (your hypothesis)

This is probably the most common.

A reduction gearbox for a 6 RPM output has an enormous gear reduction, and the last few gears transmit the highest torque. If the output shaft is prevented from turning, those gears see the greatest stress.

If one tooth breaks off:

the gears mesh normally,

the damaged tooth arrives,

the gear slips or jumps,

you hear a click or tick,

then everything continues until the damaged tooth comes around again.

If the output rotates at 6 RPM, a damaged tooth on one of the final gears would produce a regular tick every revolution or several ticks per revolution, depending on which gear is damaged.

2. A cracked gear hub
Many inexpensive gearboxes use molded nylon gears press-fit onto steel shafts.

Instead of losing teeth, the hub can split.

Under load:

the gear rotates until torque builds,

the hub slips on the shaft,

it snaps back into engagement,

producing a ticking sound.

This is another very common failure after a stall.

3. Bent shaft or displaced bearing
Less common, but if a gear shaft shifts slightly, the gears don't mesh correctly and can ratchet over one another.

4. Rotor slipping?
People sometimes describe these motors as "slipping," but a true synchronous rotor doesn't normally ratchet under normal operation.

If overloaded enough, a synchronous motor can simply lose synchronism and stop producing useful torque. That doesn't usually create a regular mechanical tick. The fact that you're hearing distinct mechanical clicking strongly points toward the gearbox rather than the electromagnetic portion of the motor.

Why didn't the motor simply burn out?
A good question.

These motors produce very little power—often only a few watts. During a stall, several things can happen before the windings overheat:

plastic gears fail,

gear hubs crack,

teeth shear,

output shafts twist,

couplings fail.

Manufacturers often prefer the gearbox to be the sacrificial component because replacing a few molded gears is cheaper than rewinding a motor (even though, in sealed units like yours, neither is really intended to be serviced).

Can the gears really survive that much reduction?
Yes. A 6 RPM output from a synchronous motor requires an overall reduction on the order of hundreds to one.

For example:

1800 RPM motor → 6 RPM output

Reduction ratio ≈ 300:1

That generally takes several gear stages. It's entirely normal to find anywhere from 5 to 10 gears inside one of these little gearmotors.

The five pins
The five protruding pins are almost certainly not electrical.

They're typically:

gear shaft supports,

riveted or press-fit shaft ends,

or bearing pins for the intermediate gears.

That matches the construction used in many inexpensive synchronous gearmotors.

That's a really interesting failure mode, and it's exactly the kind of thing that's hard to appreciate until you've built a few iterations.

The fact that you're using flats on the shaft changes the picture considerably. The set screws aren't transmitting torque by friction; they're acting as keys against the flats. That means the required clamping force is much lower than it would be on a round shaft. The problem isn't that the screws lose friction—it's that the geometry changes just enough that they stop engaging the flats effectively.

It's astonishing how little movement is required for the set screws to become irrelevant.

I believe it. Mechanically, it's a tipping point. As long as the screw tip is bearing squarely on the flat, it positively resists rotation. Once the insert or nut tilts by even a fraction of a degree, the contact patch shifts, the screw starts riding up the corner radius or loses full engagement, and the effective torque capacity collapses. It doesn't take much.

The fact that you see different implementations (captured nuts vs. heat-set inserts) failing in essentially the same way suggests the culprit isn't the hardware—it's the PETG creeping under sustained compressive load.

PETG is great for many things, but it has a reputation for exactly this behavior. Under constant stress it will slowly "flow" at room temperature. That's one reason it's often described as being tougher than PLA but less dimensionally stable under load.

What's especially challenging in your application is that Halloween props are almost the perfect environment for creep:

The coupler is under nearly constant torque while lifting the spider.

Every cycle repeats the load.

October decorations may sit in the sun during the day, and PETG's stiffness drops noticeably as temperature rises.

The mechanism spends hours cycling, giving the polymer plenty of time to relax.

None of those loads is particularly high individually, but creep doesn't require high stress if you give it enough time.

One thing your experience makes me wonder is whether the coupler itself is actually trying to solve two conflicting problems:

Be rigid enough to maintain the alignment of the threaded inserts.

Be compliant enough to become the sacrificial element.

Those goals are almost opposites.

If I were experimenting, I probably wouldn't start by changing the overload protection at all. I'd first try to eliminate creep as the dominant wear mechanism.

For example, instead of the inserts being supported only by plastic, imagine a tiny steel "bridge" between the two opposing screws. Then the plastic would only locate that bridge rather than resisting the screw forces directly. Even a thin stamped plate or a short piece of steel tubing could dramatically reduce deformation.

Another thought is to avoid point loading entirely. A split-clamp style coupler—the kind used on shaft collars—loads the plastic much more uniformly than two opposing set screws. The screws stretch the clamp instead of pushing directly into the shaft. Whether that's printable in your available space is another matter, but mechanically it's a much friendlier way to use plastic.

I also keep coming back to your original observation that the gearbox failed before the coupler.

That may simply mean the gearbox's failure torque is surprisingly low. We tend to associate "gearbox" with something robust, but the gear train inside these synchronous motors is often made from tiny acetal or nylon gears only a few millimeters across. A tooth root might be less than a millimeter thick. Even if the motor can only deliver a few watts, the reduction ratio means the last gear can see enough torque to exceed the strength of those tiny teeth.

So I don't think your coupler was "wrong." It may have been appropriately calibrated for a metal-geared DC motor and simply too slow-acting for a gearbox that has much less overload margin.

One question out of curiosity: do you know whether the failed reindeer motor had all-plastic reduction gears, or did it have a mix of metal and plastic? Some of the inexpensive synchronous gearmotors use a steel first pinion with plastic intermediate gears, while others have one or two sintered-metal gears near the output. If you could identify the gear materials, it would tell us a lot about where the designer expected the weakest link to be.

It also makes me wonder whether there's another place to introduce overload protection.

Instead of protecting the motor, perhaps protect the spool.

Suppose the spool had a tiny compliant hub—not enough to slip continuously, just enough to twist a few degrees under overload. During normal lifting, you'd never notice it. During a jam, it would absorb the initial shock before the gearbox saw the full torque.

Whether that's worth the added complexity is another question. But it's the kind of solution that addresses the distinction between shock protection and stall protection that we've uncovered in this discussion.

The progression you describe is exactly what I'd expect from a cost-optimized gearbox:

Motor shaft → steel/brass pinion (press or friction fit)

Early stages → very small nylon gears (low torque, high speed)

Middle stages → progressively larger and thicker nylon gears

Final stage → metal output gears and steel output shaft

The reason for the increasing module and thickness is that torque increases with every reduction stage. Gear tooth bending stress goes roughly as

transmitted force × tooth height ÷ tooth thickness

so the gears naturally get larger and beefier as you move toward the output.

The interesting part is the metal final gears.

At first glance, that sounds like they should make the gearbox nearly indestructible. But they also move the "weak link" upstream. If the last gear is steel and the gear driving it is nylon, then the last nylon gear becomes the sacrificial element. It's now meshing with something much stiffer than itself.

So if I had to guess where your damage occurred, I'd bet on one of the last nylon gears rather than the metal output gear.

That would also explain the ticking.

That kind of failure produces exactly the intermittent behavior you described.

The friction-fit motor pinion is interesting too. It's another deliberate weak point. If the gearbox sees a severe enough overload, the pinion can spin on the motor shaft instead of twisting the shaft itself. Manufacturers often do things like this because replacing a $0.02 molded gear or allowing a press fit to slip is preferable to damaging the motor laminations or shaft.

One thing this teardown also suggests is that these gearboxes are not designed with much overload margin. The designers have already optimized the gear sizes so that each stage carries roughly the stress it's expected to carry.
